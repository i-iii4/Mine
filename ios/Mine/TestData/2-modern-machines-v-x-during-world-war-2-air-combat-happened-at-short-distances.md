During world war 2, air combat happened at short distances. To shoot the enemy, a pilot had to point the nose of the aircraft directly at the target. 

The fastest way to do that was to pull the aircraft to a high angle relative to the airflow. 

This increased lift at first, but only up to a limit. If the pilot pulled more, the airflow over the wing broke down and the aircraft stalled.

A stalled aircraft loses lift and speed, and control becomes difficult. 

In World War 2 aircraft, this usually ended the fight because recovery took time and altitude. 

So pilots learned to stay very close to the stall, but not cross it.

Modern fighter jets can cross that limit safely. 

They can fly at a very high angle of attack, meaning the nose points steeply upward while forward speed is low. 

At this point, the wings are no longer producing normal lift. Drag is very high, and the aircraft is not flying efficiently.

However, the aircraft remains controllable because computers continuously adjust the control surfaces, and powerful engines provide enough thrust to maintain control. 

The goal is not sustained flight, but short-duration nose pointing.

Very high angle of attack exists for a single purpose:
to aim the aircraft faster than the opponent for a brief moment.

It is not better aerodynamics.
It is controlled flight after normal aerodynamics stops working.
